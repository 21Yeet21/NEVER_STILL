First, some context

Why Games Matter

Before I show you my game, a word about gaming in general. To me, games are not just entertainment — they are systems you live inside, and that changes how you think.

1Systems you inhabit

You don't watch a game — you live inside its rules. That teaches cause and effect faster than any lecture: try something, get feedback, adapt.

2The state of flow

Good games balance challenge and skill. Psychologists call it flow — full focus, lost track of time. It's the same feeling as a great study session.

3A real reset button

A short, intense game clears my head between work sessions. Not scrolling — playing. Trust me, there is a difference.

Which raises a question: what do you actually play in a five-minute break?

The origin story

Born in a Pomodoro Break

The Pomodoro technique: 25 minutes of focus, 5 minutes of break. In theory, those breaks recharge you. In practice, most of mine died in a social media feed — and I came back more tired.

So I listed what my dream break-game would need:

A full session in ~15 min — fits a break Instant start & stop — no loading, no tutorial Real progress — never a wasted run

But no game fit

Mobile games were designed to keep me, not to release me back to work. Big PC games needed an hour just to warm up.

I kept thinking: someone should build the perfect break game.

"What if a break felt like an adventure?" — so I started building one, during my breaks.
The genre

Roguelite, Explained

A roguelite is a genre named after Rogue (1980). Every time you play, it's a run: a short, self-contained life for your character. Death is not the end of your progress — it is the loop.

RRuns

Each attempt is short and self-contained — 15 to 30 minutes in my case. Die, restart, and the run is a fresh puzzle every time.

PPermadeath

No reloading an old save. When you die, that character is gone. It sounds cruel, but it makes every decision taste real.

MMeta-progression

Every run — even a failure — earns currency for permanent upgrades. This is what makes it "lite": progress softens the sting.

Famous examples: Hades· Dead Cells· Vampire Survivors
Inspiration Nº1

Vampire Survivors

A phenomenon sold for five dollars: hundreds of monsters flood the screen, and you have exactly one job — move. Attacks fire automatically. The controls disappear, and pure game feel remains.

It proved something important: simplicity is not a limitation — it's the design.

What I borrowed

▸ Huge crowds — hundreds of enemies at once, so performance became an obsession of mine.

▸ One-line controls — if you can explain it in one breath, players stay.

▸ Juice — every single kill must feel crunchy and rewarding.

▸ Build variety — new combo every run.

Inspiration Nº2

Shape of Dreams

An action roguelite where movement is the combat: you dash through enemies, chain repositions, and the characters are bold, readable silhouettes you can parse in the middle of chaos.

It asked a question that stuck with me: what if dodging felt as powerful as attacking?

What I borrowed

▸ Motion as identity — how you move should define how the game feels.

▸ Sculptural enemies — readable at a glance, even with fifty things on screen.

▸ Speed is rewarded, never punished.

That idea — motion as a weapon — became the engine of NEVER STILL.

Core mechanic Nº2

The Hush

A frozen wall that is always advancing behind you. It never speeds up to catch you — but it never, ever stops. Touch it and your health starts to freeze away.

THE HUSH
safe distance — always shrinking

1No camping

There is no safe corner, no hideout. Every second you spend standing still is a second the wall gets closer.

2Route, don't react

The Hush turns the map into a routing puzzle: plan your path through enemies instead of fighting in place.

3Bosses can't hide

When a boss lags too far behind, the Hush teleports it forward — even Warlords must keep up with you.

Post-mortem

What Building It Taught Me

1Motion is emotion

How you move is how a game feels. Tuning speed curves taught me more about game feel than any art asset ever did.

2Scope small, polish big

One mechanic pushed to its limit beats ten half-built ones. I cut more features than I kept — and the game got better every time.

3Playtest ugly

My spreadsheet said a mechanic was balanced. Five minutes of my sister playing proved it was a lie. Test early, test ugly.

And yes — my Pomodoro breaks are now dangerously fun. Discipline is harder than game design.
